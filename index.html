Chapter 1 — Origins

The National School Lunch Program was established by the National School Lunch Act of 1946 (Public Law 79-396), signed June 4, 1946. Its stated purpose was twofold: "as a measure of national security, to safeguard the health and well-being of the Nation's children and to encourage the domestic consumption of nutritious agricultural commodities." Both halves of that sentence still shape how money moves — nutrition and farm policy were fused at birth. (Draft opening — verify quotations against the statute before submission.)

Precedent examined — Drone Wars

Before building, the team examined Drone Wars, a prior UCLA introductory Digital Humanities final project (Fall 2018, directed by Dr. Ashley Sanders Garcia and Craig Messner). It is a data-driven narrative about the U.S. drone campaign in Afghanistan, Pakistan, Somalia, and Yemen, built from Bureau of Investigative Journalism datasets, and it is one of the past DH 101 final projects the course itself points students to as an example.

Three structural choices in this site refer back to it. First, the project is organised narrative-first, with data visualisations enfolded into an argument rather than presented as a gallery. Second, the methodology above accounts for the work in three levels — sources, processing, presentation — the same three-layer accounting Drone Wars used on its Resources page. Third, following that Resources page, this site places its Bibliography last: in the navigation, in the Contents list, in the Continue links, and in the document itself, so the complete list of works cited closes the project.

Two things were deliberately not followed. Drone Wars was built with Mobirise, a drag-and-drop website builder; this course prohibits builder tools, so this site is hand-coded HTML, CSS, and JavaScript instead. And none of Drone Wars’ data, text, or team is reused here — which matters, because research for this reference identified the unnamed roster in the comparison draft evaluated during this build: its four names are members of the Drone Wars team, carried over without attribution. They were rejected as this team’s roster at the time because they could not be verified as the actual team; they are now positively identified as someone else’s.

Data Critique

Ontology, inclusions, and blind spots

A revised critique of the comparison dataset: what it contains, what it can illuminate, what it cannot, and the ideological effects of how its sources divided the world into data. Placeholder structure with the framing worked out — the team revises this against the real dataset before submission.

✎ On the board: what this dataset can — and cannot — say

What is included

One row per item (state) with a total allocation, an eligible-student count, a reporting period, and a named source, from which a per-student value is computed. Every figure on the Data page derives from these five columns and nothing else — which is precisely the limitation the rest of this page examines.

per-student = total ÷ eligible — but ≠ quality, ≠ need, ≠ experience

What it can illuminate

Differences in the per-student measure across states, the shape of their distribution (Figure 2), outliers worth individual review, and — once multiple periods are loaded — change over time. It can also show where reporting itself is uneven: the data-quality panel counts what is missing.

What it cannot show

Meal quality, student experience, participation among the eligible, need among the ineligible, and causes. A gap in the per-student measure is a question, not a verdict — the narrative is required to treat it that way.

Ontology and ideological effects

Dividing the program into "allocations," "eligible students," and "per-student values" adopts the administering agencies' own categories. That choice makes states commensurable and ranks them, while rendering invisible everything the categories exclude: children just above the eligibility line, districts that feed everyone under universal-meals policies, and the agricultural half of the program's founding purpose. A revised critique must name whose categories these are, what they flatten, and what a dataset built from different categories would make visible instead.
